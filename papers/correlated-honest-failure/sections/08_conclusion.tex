\section{Discussion and conclusion}

The standard honest/adversarial partition is sufficient only when ``honest'' also means ``independently correct for the fault being exploited.'' Correlated implementation failure separates those concepts.

The resulting JAM/ELVES analysis has three nested event-level boundaries, an orthogonal cross-time maintenance condition, and a dynamic bridge between them.

First, the dispute layer has an attribution boundary. If the combined adversarial-plus-buggy-positive bloc exceeds roughly one third of the validator population, independently correct validators no longer suffice by themselves to construct a bad verdict. The report can still be stopped through a wonky outcome, but the ordinary Gray Paper offender-attribution path changes. This is a protocol-rule result, not a branching-model result.

Second, the audit layer has a correction boundary. Under the ELVES pessimistic branching extension,
\[
\lambda_x=Fh_x,
\]
and correction ceases to be supercritical at \(h_x=1/F\). For \(F=2\), the corresponding wrong-positive boundary is one half, above the one-third attribution threshold.

Third, sampled correction introduces dilution. With
\[
\lambda_x=F\frac{C_x}{N},
\]
validator growth can reduce corrective reproduction when new validators do not increase the independently correct set for the relevant fault. This can occur even if every entrant is honest and the adversarial fraction falls.

Fourth, event-level correction is not the same as preserving the capacity to remain corrective across future audits. The declared slow maintenance model separates independently corrective capacity, maintenance observability or attribution, and returned resources. Its canonical three-cycle threshold is
\[
k_{CO}k_{OR}k_{RC}\ge(1-r_C)(1-r_O)(1-r_R).
\]
Machine-checked counterexamples show that this maintenance condition and \(\lambda_x>1\) imply neither one another. The maintenance condition should therefore not be read as a fourth point on the earlier one-third/one-half/two-thirds sequence. It is an orthogonal timescale.

Fifth, once the slow corrective-capacity state is allowed to feed the next audit, the separation becomes a dynamic resilience condition. Under
\[
C_{t+1}=r_CC_t+k_{RC}R_t,
\qquad
\lambda_{x,t}=FC_t,
\]
we obtain
\[
\lambda_{x,t+1}=r_C\lambda_{x,t}+Fk_{RC}R_t.
\]
At current criticality this gives the exact machine-checked boundary
\[
\boxed{
\lambda_{x,t+1}\ge1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
Fk_{RC}R_t\ge1-r_C.
}
\]
The difference
\[
M_t=Fk_{RC}R_t-(1-r_C)
\]
is therefore a candidate local maintenance-return margin: its sign tells whether a currently critical corrective state crosses below, stays at, or moves above the fast audit threshold on the next slow update.

This dynamic bridge exposes a stronger failure mode than static insufficiency. A system can remain on the supercritical side of its current audit condition while its corrective margin is predictably decaying toward subcriticality. The Lean formalization demonstrates this by constructing two systems with the same current \(\lambda_0=3/2\) but opposite five-step threshold status under different reduced maintenance multipliers. Current audit reproduction is therefore not, within the declared model, a sufficient statistic for finite-horizon corrective resilience.

Together these results suggest that the relevant security object is not raw validator count, raw client count, or even current \(\lambda_x\) alone. It is a pair consisting of fault-specific independent corrective capacity and the maintenance dynamics that replenish or erode that capacity. Because future faults are unknown, this object cannot be observed perfectly. But implementation exposure, useful audit effort, client/operator turnover, diversity-aware rewards, development resources and other return flows can in principle provide measurable proxies.

The most concise hierarchy is therefore:
\[
\boxed{
\text{honest stake}
\neq
\text{independently corrective stake}
\neq
\text{attacker-attributable stake},
}
\]
followed by
\[
\boxed{
\text{corrective reproduction now}
\neq
\text{reproduction of corrective capacity over time}
\neq
\text{finite-horizon corrective resilience}.
}
\]

The first distinction matters for auditing and economics. The second separates an event-level success condition from a cross-time maintenance condition. The third makes that maintenance condition operational by asking whether the observed return flows are sufficient to keep the fast corrective threshold from being crossed in the future.

This also clarifies the relationship to existing client-diversity work. Demonstrating or rewarding minority implementations can improve the observability and resource-return parts of the loop, but it does not by itself establish that fault-specific independent corrective capacity is above replacement. The additional question is whether the resulting return process is strong and fast enough relative to attrition to preserve future correction.

External review by the ELVES and JAM authors remains essential, especially on the wonky-verdict economics, the substitution of \(h_x\) into the branching proof, and the mapping from protocol/ecosystem mechanisms to the declared slow state. The strongest next empirical test is correspondingly concrete: determine whether a JAM deployment or testnet can estimate both a current fault-domain correction margin and an independent maintenance-return margin, then test whether the latter predicts changes in the former before event-level correction becomes subcritical.
